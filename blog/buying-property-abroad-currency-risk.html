Buying abroad in 2026: how to protect yourself from currency swings

14 July 2026
4 min read
Dinheiro Research Team

Buying a property abroad is one of the largest financial decisions most people will ever make. And for UK buyers purchasing in euros, the exchange rate on the day of completion is one of the most significant variables in the entire transaction — yet it's also one of the least planned for.

Most buyers are focused, quite rightly, on finding the right property, navigating the local legal system, and managing surveyors, solicitors and mortgage arrangements. Currency tends to be left until the last minute. That's understandable — but it's expensive.

The numbers are bigger than you think

Let's put some figures to it. GBP/EUR has moved by more than 6% in both directions within a single calendar year in three of the last five years. On a €500,000 purchase — a fairly typical budget for a family home in southern France, coastal Spain or the Algarve — a 3% adverse move in the exchange rate costs you approximately £13,000 in additional sterling outlay. A 6% move costs you around £26,000.

That's before we consider the hidden cost of using your bank. High street banks typically apply a 2.5–3.5% spread over the interbank rate on international transfers. On the same €500,000 purchase, that's another £12,000–18,000 going straight to your bank in margin — margin you'd never see on a statement, but pay nonetheless.

Combined — rate movement and bank margin — the total unplanned cost on a typical overseas property purchase can easily reach £30,000–40,000. Most buyers have no idea this is happening.

The three tools available to you

1. A forward contract

A forward contract lets you fix today's exchange rate for a transfer that will happen at a future date — typically up to 12 months ahead. Once you know your completion date and the amount in euros, you lock the rate and then settle at that rate on the agreed day, regardless of where the market has moved in the meantime.

It costs nothing to set up. You typically put down a small deposit (usually 5–10% of the transfer value), and the balance is settled on completion. If the rate moves in your favour, you've missed out on that upside — but if it moves against you, you're fully protected. For a purchase of this scale, most buyers find the certainty is worth far more than the option value.

2. A limit order

If you have some flexibility on timing and the rate is currently below where you'd ideally like to transact, a limit order lets you set a target rate and instructs us to execute automatically when the market reaches it. You don't need to watch the market constantly — we do that for you and execute the moment your rate is achieved.

3. A combination approach

Some buyers choose to hedge part of their exposure with a forward contract and leave part open, hoping to benefit from favourable movement while protecting a floor. This is a reasonable strategy when you have genuine budget flexibility and a longer timeline to completion.

The timeline that catches buyers out

One of the most common mistakes is leaving the currency conversation until after exchange of contracts — sometimes just days before completion. At that point, you have no choice but to transact at whatever the market is doing that day, through whatever institution you can reach in time.

The ideal timeline looks like this: once your offer is accepted, even before exchange, speak to your currency provider. That's the moment you have the most flexibility to put a structure in place. If exchange is months away, you might use a limit order to try to capture a better rate. Once exchange happens, many buyers lock in with a forward contract for the completion balance.

The golden rule: don't wait until completion week. By then, your options are limited and the pressure to transact — at any rate — is enormous.
