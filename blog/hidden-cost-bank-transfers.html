The hidden cost of paying international suppliers through your bank

9 July 2026
3 min read
Dinheiro Research Team

Ask most business owners how much they pay to send money internationally and they'll tell you about the transfer fee — typically £15–30 per transaction. What they don't realise is that the transfer fee is the smallest part of what they're actually paying.

The real cost is hidden inside the exchange rate itself. And for businesses making regular international payments, it adds up to a sum that would make most finance directors wince.

How the bank margin works

When you ask your bank to convert pounds into euros — or dollars, or any other currency — they don't give you the interbank rate. The interbank rate is the rate at which banks trade with each other, the true mid-market price. Your bank applies a spread on top of this rate: they buy low and sell high, pocketing the difference.

For retail and business customers, that spread is typically 2.5–3.5% above the interbank rate. Some banks are worse. You won't see this figure on your statement — it's baked silently into the rate you're quoted.

On a £50,000 payment to a foreign supplier, a 3% bank spread costs you approximately £1,500 — per transaction. A business making this payment monthly is losing over £18,000 a year to bank margin alone.

What this looks like across your payment schedule

For businesses with regular international commitments — supplier invoices, overseas payroll, software licences billed in USD, logistics costs in euros — the cumulative impact is significant. A company with a monthly international payment book of £100,000 could be paying £25,000–35,000 a year more than necessary, purely in bank margin.

That's not a rounding error. For many businesses, it's equivalent to a part-time member of staff, a marketing budget, or a meaningful chunk of capital expenditure.

Why most businesses don't notice

The margin is invisible because it's embedded in the rate. If your bank quotes you a conversion rate and you accept it, there's no line item on the confirmation that says "margin: £1,500." The money simply isn't there when it arrives on the other side.

For businesses that have always used their bank for international payments, this has become a normalised cost — one they've never questioned because they've never seen it broken down. Many of our clients tell us they assumed the bank margin was "just how it worked." It isn't.

Beyond the rate: what else banks get wrong

Margin aside, there are other ways in which bank infrastructure is poorly suited to international payment needs. Settlement times are slower — a same-day transfer through a specialist provider often takes two to three days through a bank. The ability to forward-fix rates, use limit orders, or manage currency exposure in a structured way is either absent or cumbersome through high street banking relationships. And the level of service — a dedicated relationship manager who understands your business and your currencies — simply doesn't exist.

These are meaningful operational disadvantages on top of the cost issue, particularly for businesses where the timing of a payment matters as much as the cost.
